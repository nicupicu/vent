Neonatal Pulmonary Graphics

Learn to read the live pictures a ventilator draws of every breath your tiny patient takes — waveforms, loops and the numbers behind them.

📈

Why graphics matter

Think of the ventilator screen as a live “heart monitor” for breathing: it shows, breath after breath, how the baby and the machine are working together.

Real-time feedback

Graphical user interfaces (GUIs) give you a constant, breath-by-breath view of the conversation between the infant and the ventilator. You do not have to wait for a blood gas to learn that something has changed.

A great teacher

Because you can literally see what a setting does, graphics are an excellent way to learn ventilation. Change a setting, watch the curve change.

From add-on to built-in

For roughly the last decade, graphics came as an optional monitor you could add to a ventilator. The newest ventilators now have colour touch-screens built right in.

💡 Plain-language explainer

A waveform plots one thing (pressure, flow or volume) against time. A loop plots one thing against another (for example pressure against volume) so you see how they relate.

🎯

When do we use graphics?

Graphics help in four big jobs: tuning the ventilator, judging the baby’s own effort, checking if a drug worked, and reading the lungs themselves.

A. Tune the ventilator

  • Peak inspiratory pressure (PIP)
  • Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP)
  • Inspiratory or expiratory tidal volume (VTI or VTE)
  • Inspiratory time (TI) and expiratory time (TE)
  • Flow rate
  • Synchronization with the baby
  • Compliance

B. Judge spontaneous effort

  • The baby’s own tidal volume (VT)
  • Minute ventilation (MV)
  • Breathing pattern
  • Readiness for extubation

C. Check drug response

  • Surfactant
  • Bronchodilators
  • Diuretics
  • Steroids

D. Read waves, loops & mechanics

  • Waveforms: pressure, flow, volume
  • Loops: pressure–volume and flow–volume
  • Mechanics: dynamic or static compliance, inspiratory and expiratory resistance, time constants

E. Evaluating the disease

Graphics help you tell apart restrictive problems (stiff lungs), obstructive problems (narrowed airways), how severe things are, and whether the baby is recovering.

Graphical user interfaces (GUIs)

A GUI is simply the screen that draws your baby’s breathing as it happens.

What it does

Shows a continuous, real-time, breath-to-breath picture of how patient and ventilator interact.

Teaching value

It is an excellent teaching tool — students see cause and effect immediately.

Hardware story

Graphics monitors have been an add-on option for about a decade. The latest generation of ventilators has integral colour touch-screens.

🔬

Where and how flow is measured

Every graph starts with a sensor. If the sensor is in the wrong place, the picture lies.

✅ Proximal sensor

Sits right at the airway opening (the connection to the endotracheal tube). This position is critical for accurate waveforms, loops and numbers.

⚠️ Distal sensor

Sits inside the ventilator or near where the tubing leaves it. Its readings include the stretch (compliance) and resistance of the whole breathing circuit, not just the baby.

⚠️ Clinical caution

The so-called “circuit compliance compensation” maths does not correct the displayed data accurately. Do not use it in very preterm babies, because under-delivering volume to them may promote lung injury.

💡 Plain-language explainer

Imagine measuring how much water a child drinks by checking the tap in the kitchen instead of the cup at their lips: some water is lost in the pipes. A distal sensor does the same with air.

Three types of flow sensor

🔥 Heated-wire anemometer

A thin wire is kept at a steady temperature. Passing gas cools it, so the sensor measures how much electric current is needed to keep it warm. That current converts to flow, and adding up flow over time gives volume.

↔️ Differential-pressure pneumotachometer

Gas flows across an element in the sensor, creating a pressure difference between the upstream and downstream ports. That pressure change is proportional to flow.

⚡ Diaphragmatic neural sensor

Uses a modified feeding tube carrying several electrodes that pick up the diaphragm’s electrical activity (diaphragmatic EMG) — it reads the baby’s breathing drive rather than airflow.

The pressure waveform

Pressure on the vertical axis, time along the bottom. It is the first trace to learn.

What you’re seeing (Fig. 22.1, top trace)

  • Up-sweep = inspiration (air going in).
  • Down-sweep = expiration (air coming out).
  • Letter A = the highest point = PIP.
  • Letter B = the baseline = PEEP.
  • The shape tells you the breath type: triangular = volume breath, square = pressure breath.

Mean airway pressure

The shaded area under the pressure curve represents the mean airway pressure — the average pressure the lungs feel over the whole breath cycle. A wider, taller shaded area means a higher average.

💡 Plain-language explainer

It is like the average water level in a bath that keeps filling and draining: the more time spent high, the higher the average.

🌬️

The flow waveform

Flow shows how fast gas moves — in or out — at every instant.

What you’re seeing (Fig. 22.1, middle trace)

  • The horizontal line is zero flow.
  • Above the line = inspiratory flow; below = expiratory flow.
  • Letter C = biggest upward swing = peak inspiratory flow.
  • Letter D = biggest downward swing = peak expiratory flow.
  • Letter E = inspiratory time, measured from the moment flow starts until expiratory flow begins.
  • Letter F = expiratory time, from the moment expiratory flow starts until the next inspiration begins. When expiratory flow returns to zero (arrow), the lung has finished emptying.

Flow at zero: nothing more can go in

Once flow reaches zero (arrows), no extra volume can be delivered to the baby.

  • Panel a: inspiratory time is set so inspiration ends just as flow returns to zero.
  • Panel b: inspiratory time is too long. A pressure plateau appears — pressure is held but no new volume goes in — and mean airway pressure rises.

Flow cycling

With flow cycling, the breath switches into expiration by a rule, usually when flow falls to about 5–25 % of peak inspiratory flow. Letting the baby help decide when inspiration ends can improve synchronization. It is available on newer ventilators and on any ventilator with pressure support.

  • Red = inspiration; blue = expiration.
  • The arrow marks the end of inspiration while flow is still above the zero line.

Gas trapping

If expiratory flow has not returned to zero before the next breath starts (arrow), air may be getting trapped in the lungs.

⚠️ Clinical caution: gas trapping

The expiratory time is too short for the lung to empty. Look for an expiratory trace that is cut off before reaching the zero line.

🧪

The volume waveform

Volume is the total amount of gas moved — flow added up over time.

What you’re seeing (Fig. 22.1, bottom trace)

  • Sweeping upward = inspiration; sweeping downward = expiration.
  • The red line marks the delivered inspiratory tidal volume.

Endotracheal tube leak

When the expiratory part of the volume waveform fails to come back to the zero baseline (arrow), some gas is escaping around the tube. The flow trace in this figure shows the same story: expiratory flow does not fully return to baseline before the next breath.

⚠️ Clinical caution: ET-tube leak

Volume that goes in but doesn’t come back out means measurements of exhaled volume will be lower than what was delivered.

💡 Plain-language explainer

Traditional volume ventilation delivers gas at a constant speed, so the flow trace looks like a square block. Some ventilators let you make that flow decelerate (taper off) instead.

🤝

Baby and ventilator, together

Good ventilation means the machine’s breaths match the baby’s own effort.

Unsynchronized IMV

Intermittent mandatory ventilation (IMV) was the first neonatal mode that let babies breathe between machine breaths. But the machine breaths land at random points in the baby’s own breathing cycle, which can cause harm — including pneumothorax and intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH).

  • Yellow arrows: the baby’s effort adds to the ventilator breath, giving a very high VT.
  • Green arrows: the baby starts to breathe out while the machine is still pushing air in.

Synchronized IMV (SIMV)

SIMV times the machine breath to the baby’s own inspiratory effort, so they work together instead of colliding.

  • Yellow arrows: synchronized machine breath.
  • Red arrows: the baby’s own breath, with no machine help.

Assist/control (A/C)

A/C assists by starting a machine breath whenever the baby tries to breathe, and controls by delivering a time-cycled breath if the baby is apneic or fails to trigger.

  • Red: time-cycled machine breath during apnea.
  • Yellow: breaths started by the patient.

Pressure support

Pressure support is very similar to A/C: every breath the patient triggers is supported by a set pressure. The difference is that it can also be blended into SIMV as a combined mode (shown here).

The pressure–volume (P–V) loop

This loop shows how much volume you get for each unit of pressure — the lung’s “stretchiness”.

What you’re seeing

  • Horizontal axis: pressure. Vertical axis: volume.
  • Inspiration sweeps up from the baseline (PEEP) to PIP.
  • Expiration sweeps back down from PIP to baseline.
  • A line drawn between the two end-points shows compliance (ΔV/ΔP): steeper means stretchier lungs.

Is PEEP enough?

PEEP keeps some air in the lungs at the end of expiration (end-expiratory lung volume). If the inspiratory limb has a lower inflection point — a bend that marks the opening pressure — then PEEP is inadequate.

Overdistension

If the top of the inspiratory limb flattens, extra pressure is being applied with no extra volume — the lung is over-stretched. Most neonatal ventilators report this as the C20/C ratio.

💡 Plain-language explainer

Blowing up a balloon: at first it’s hard, then easy, then at the end you push hard and it hardly grows. That last stage is overdistension.

Is flow high enough?

If the ventilator isn’t supplying flow fast enough, the inspiratory side looks dented or scooped (cusping). Severe flow limitation can twist the loop into a figure-8.

🩺 At the bedside

A cusped or figure-8 loop is a clue that the baby wants more flow than the machine is giving.

🔄

The flow–volume (F–V) loop

This loop shows how fast air moves at each point of the breath, so it is great for airway resistance.

What you’re seeing

  • Horizontal axis: volume. Vertical axis: flow.
  • The breath starts at zero, moves up and to the right during inspiration, ending at the delivered inspiratory volume.
  • On expiration it travels down and to the left, back to zero.

Resistance changes the shape

  • Increased inspiratory resistance (Fig. 22.16): the inspiratory limb is flattened.
  • Increased expiratory resistance (Fig. 22.17): the expiratory limb is flattened.
💡 Plain-language explainer

Breathing through a narrow straw squashes the flow peak, so the loop looks flat.

Did the bronchodilator work?

The loop helps check whether a bronchodilator is helping airway reactivity. In Fig. 22.18, loop B (after treatment) shows higher inspiratory and expiratory flow than loop A (before).

Secretions or water

Secretions or water in the tubing or flow sensor show up as changes on the loop display. Suctioning should only be done when indicated, so the loop is a useful way to decide when to suction or drain water from the circuit. Fig. 22.19 shows an infant who needs suctioning.

🩺 At the bedside

A messy loop can be your cue to check the circuit and airway before changing ventilator settings.

Measurements and calculations

The numbers behind the pictures. Worked examples below use made-up values to practise the arithmetic; the normal ranges are from the chapter.

Tidal volume (VT)

Measured on inspiration and on expiration. Normal delivered VT is 4–7 mL/kg.

Example: 3 kg baby → 12 to 21 mL.

Minute ventilation (MV)

MV = VT × rate

Normal range 240–360 mL/kg/min.

Example: 5 mL/kg × 50 breaths/min = 250 mL/kg/min (in range).

Peak vs. static pressure

Pressure can be read as peak inspiratory pressure or as static pressure. Static pressure comes from an inflation-hold: the exhalation valve is closed and flow stops during a machine breath, so you read the pressure with nothing moving.

💡 Plain-language explainer: compliance & resistance

Compliance = how stretchy a balloon is (how much volume for each bit of pressure). A stiff lung is low-compliance. Resistance = how narrow a drinking straw is (how much pressure to push a given flow). A narrow airway is high-resistance.

🧮 Formula: dynamic compliance
CD = VTiPIP − PEEP
  • CD: compliance based on peak pressure.
  • VTi: inspiratory tidal volume.
  • PIP − PEEP: the pressure swing used to inflate the lung.
Example: VTi 12 mL, PIP 20, PEEP 5 → 12 ÷ 15 = 0.8 mL/cmH2O.
🧮 Formula: static compliance
CST = VTiPST − PEEP
  • CST: compliance based on static pressure.
  • PST: static pressure from the inflation hold.
Example: VTi 12 mL, PST 18, PEEP 5 → 12 ÷ 13 ≈ 0.92 mL/cmH2O.
🧮 C20/C ratio

The compliance of the last 20 % of the P–V curve divided by the compliance of the whole curve. With overdistension this ratio is below 1.0.

Example: a reading of 0.7 means the end of the breath is much stiffer than the start → suspect overdistension.
🧮 Formula: airway resistance
RAW = PIP − PEEPFlow   (cmH2O/L/s)

Resistance links pressure and flow. The pressure can be dynamic or static, and flow can be taken in three ways:

  • Peak flow: the maximum flow in inspiration or expiration.
  • Average flow: based on multiple-point linear regression.
  • Mid-volume flow: the flow measured at the halfway point of volume delivery.
Example: PIP 20, PEEP 5, flow 0.5 L/s → 15 ÷ 0.5 = 30 cmH2O/L/s.
